Retail arbitrage on Walmart: how it works

Retail arbitrage on Walmart means buying products in physical stores (clearance aisles, seasonal markdowns, closeouts at Target, Walgreens, Walmart itself and others) and reselling them on Walmart Marketplace for more. It's the most common way new Walmart sellers start, because you can begin with a few hundred dollars and a phone.

The catch is that the price on the shelf isn't your profit. Walmart takes a referral fee (15% in most categories), WFS charges a fulfillment fee by weight if Walmart ships for you, and you still have to beat the other sellers on the listing. A $20 clearance item that sells for $34 can look like $14 of profit and end up closer to $5.

What to check before you buy

  • Profit after all fees, not just the price difference. Margenos subtracts referral and WFS fees for you.
  • ROI. Many resellers aim for 30% or more so there's room if the price drops.
  • Sales speed. Walmart has no public sales rank, so Margenos estimates monthly sales from Walmart's own signals.
  • Competition. How many sellers are on the listing, how much stock they have, and whether Walmart itself sells it.
  • Restrictions. Some brands and categories need approval on Walmart; check before you buy a lot.

Walmart online arbitrage works in two directions

"Walmart online arbitrage" can mean two different businesses:

  • Selling on Walmart: buying products cheaply anywhere (other websites, stores, wholesale) and selling them on Walmart Marketplace. That's what this page and Margenos are built for.
  • Buying from Walmart: buying Walmart.com deals or clearance to resell on another marketplace, or on Walmart itself.

The second one has rules worth knowing before you build a business on it.

Walmart's rules on buying for resale

Walmart.com's terms of use say Walmart may limit the quantity of items bought per person, per household or per order, may refuse or cancel any order, and reserves the right to prohibit sales to dealers or resellers. The terms define reselling as buying products from Walmart to sell them commercially. In practice that means large online orders of a hot deal can get cut or cancelled. Spread your buying across many products, don't count on any single deal, and only treat inventory as yours once it ships.

Buying at Walmart and reselling on Walmart Marketplace

Yes, resellers do buy Walmart clearance and sell it back on Walmart.com. It can work, because store clearance is often priced well below the online listing, but it comes with a specific problem: you're often competing with Walmart itself, and Walmart tends to win the Buy Box on its own listings. The deals that work usually share one trait: Walmart is out of stock online or not selling the item at all.

Walmart retail arbitrage: rules on the selling side

  • Don't overprice sold-out items. Walmart's pricing rules unpublish offers priced far above recent prices on Walmart.com or competing sites. Price against recent history, which the Walmart price tracker shows.
  • Keep receipts and invoices. If a brand or listing is ever questioned, proof of purchase is what you have to show. Some brands and categories also need approval.
  • Count sales, not just margin. A big spread on a slow item ties up cash. Check the sales estimate and slow-seller warnings.
  • Price the whole cart. On the top plan, CartIQ shows the profit on everything in your cart before you check out.

You can, and some resellers do with store clearance. The challenge is that Walmart often sells the same item and wins the Buy Box, so the best deals are usually items Walmart is out of stock on online.
